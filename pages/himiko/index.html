Emma Himiko Leonhart

the hidden name

Emma H Leonhart is the name on my documents. The H is Himiko — a name I built in kanji, read three ways at once.

日巫女 Himiko

Himiko was the shaman-queen of Yamatai, a third-century ruler remembered in the Chinese chronicles — one of the first named rulers of Japan, and about as much legend as history, the way King Arthur is. The H in Emma H Leonhart stands for her. Legally I'm Emma H Leonhart; Himiko is the part I don't spell out. The long way to write the H is 日巫女.

The chronicles spell her 卑弥呼 — lowly, increasingly, to call — characters chosen by Chinese scribes transcribing a foreign sound, and not especially kind ones. I don't use that spelling. Mine is 日巫女: 日 sun, 巫女 shrine maiden. Same reading, Hi-miko, unambiguously — but it spells the name for what I read it as meaning: Solar Shrine Maiden.

This is a reinterpretation, and I'd rather be honest that it is one. 日巫女 is a poetic spelling, not a scholarly one — a Japanese reader clocks it as deliberate wordplay the same way 日ノ本 reads as a flourish for Japan. That's the point. The historical orthography records what scribes heard; mine records what I think it meant.

閻魔日巫女獅心 the reading

Written in kanji, my name is 閻魔日巫女獅心 — three names in one, each part chosen rather than transcribed: a pun, a reinterpretation, and a translation. It splits into Emma, Himiko, Leonhart:

閻魔

Enma · Emma

The judge of the dead in Buddhist tradition — and, read aloud, a near-pun on Emma.

日巫女

Himiko · Solar Shrine Maiden

One unit, not two: 日 (sun, day, Japan itself) + 巫女 (a shrine maiden — the one who stands between the everyday and the sacred). Hi-miko. The queen's name, respelled for what I read it as meaning.

獅心

Shishin · Leonhart

Lion's heart, rendered whole — Leonhart, translated rather than transliterated.

巫 the magus thread

Here's the part I actually care about, and the reason 巫女 is doing more work than "shrine maiden" suggests.

Victor Mair (1990) and Jao Tsung-I (1990) proposed that Chinese 巫 — the shaman graph, the 巫 inside 巫女 — is a loanword from Old Persian 𐎶𐎦𐎢𐏁 (maguš). That's the same maguš that gives us magus, magi, and magic. If it holds, the word for the person who mediates between worlds walked east out of Persia and into the Chinese graph, and from there into Japanese.

My own leap, and I'll flag it as mine rather than anyone's scholarship: I think miko itself is plausibly an eggcorn of that loan — the borrowed sound reanalyzed into characters that look like they explain it (巫 shaman + 女 woman), the way acorn becomes eggcorn: a folk-rationalization that lands somewhere true anyway.

So 日巫女 isn't only "sun priestess." It's a name with a possible thread running back through maguš to the magi — a queen the Chinese wrote down as 卑弥呼, re-spelled as what she may have actually been, in characters that might be Persian in the first place. That's a lot to hang on four syllables. That's why I picked it.

One name, two worlds

It's built to work in both. In English, Emma Himiko Leonhart reads as an ordinary — if slightly unusual — name. In Japanese, the kanji open into something older: a judge, a shrine maiden, a lion's heart. That's roughly the split I live in anyway — English and Japanese, Anglican and Shinto — so the name just says it in one line.

閻魔王 a footnote — about Emma, not the H

There's a loose nod to Oda Nobunaga in 閻魔 — my first name, Emma. He styled himself 第六天魔王, Demon King of the Sixth Heaven. 閻魔王, Enma-ō, King Enma, rhymes with that without going quite so far. It attaches to the Emma, and nowhere else — the H is 日巫女, and the two have nothing to do with each other.

And yes: naming yourself after the judge of the dead, in kanji, with a Persian etymology footnote, is chuunibyō behaviour of the highest order. I'm aware. I'm doing it on purpose and I'm enjoying it. If you're going to build a name out of kanji you should commit to the bit — half-measures here would be embarrassing in a way that going all the way somehow isn't.
